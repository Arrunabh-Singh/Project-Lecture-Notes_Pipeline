# Lenses: Types and Terms

**NCERT sections covered:** 9.5.2


## What is a lens? (NCERT 9.5.2)

**Lens (board):** a transparent material bounded by two surfaces, of which one or both are spherical, forms a lens. At least one surface has to be spherical: with both surfaces plane you get a slab, not a lens.

### Convex (converging) lens

- **Both surfaces spherical and bulging outwards**. It is **thicker at the centre** than at the edges.
- Called a **double convex lens**, or simply a **convex lens**. If one face is plane, it is a **plano-convex** lens.
- Parallel rays falling on it **converge** to a point (F) after refraction, so a convex lens is a **converging lens**.

### Concave (diverging) lens

- **Both surfaces spherical and curved inwards**. It is **thicker at the edges** than at the centre.
- Called a **double concave lens**, or simply a **concave lens**. If one face is plane, it is a **plano-concave** lens.
- Parallel rays falling on it **diverge** after refraction, as if coming from one point (F), so a concave lens is a **diverging lens**.

### Meniscus lenses (an extra from the lecture)
When one face bulges out and the other curves in:

- thicker at the centre: **concavo-convex** lens;
- thinner at the centre: **convexo-concave** lens.

*(The teacher notes these are not in the textbook.)*

## Terms related to a lens

- **Centres of curvature ($C_1$, $C_2$).** Each of a lens's two spherical surfaces is part of a sphere. The centres of those spheres are $C_1$ and $C_2$.
- **Radii of curvature ($R_1$, $R_2$).** The radii of those two spheres. *(The teacher says this definition isn't spelled out in the textbook, so it is built from the C₁/C₂ one.)*
- **Principal axis.** The line passing through the two centres of curvature, $C_1$ and $C_2$. Compare a mirror, which has only one spherical surface and so one C.
- **Optical centre (O).** The central point of a lens. **A ray passing through the optical centre goes undeviated.**
    - This holds only for a **thin lens**. Slice a thin lens into pieces: the central piece is effectively a very thin parallel-sided slab. A slab only displaces a ray sideways, and with negligible thickness the lateral shift is negligible, so the ray appears to pass straight through.
- **Aperture.** The effective diameter of the circular outline of a spherical lens (MN on the board). For a concave lens it is measured across the rim, not the thin middle.
- **Thin lens.** A lens whose aperture is much smaller than its radius of curvature. All the lens results here assume thin lenses.

## Principal focus and focal length

**Convex lens.** When several rays parallel to the principal axis fall on a convex lens, after refraction they **converge** at a point on the principal axis. This point is the **principal focus** of the lens.

**Concave lens.** When several rays parallel to the principal axis fall on a concave lens, after refraction they **appear to diverge** from a point on the principal axis. This point is the **principal focus**.

**Focal length ($f$).** The distance between the optical centre and the principal focus.

**Drawing convention:** for a thin lens, draw the bending of each ray at the **centre line** of the lens, not at the two surfaces separately.

**Two principal foci.** If parallel rays come from the other side of the lens, they focus on the opposite side. So a lens has **two principal foci, $F_1$ and $F_2$** ("focus" is singular, "foci" plural). This answers "why does a lens have two principal foci?"

- $OF_1 = OF_2$ **only if the medium on both sides of the lens is the same**, for example air | glass ($n = 1.5$) | air.
- With air on one side and water on the other, the two focal lengths differ.

*(This last point is an extra from the lecture. In the course, both sides are always the same medium.)*
