# Spherical Mirrors: Pole, Centre of Curvature, Principal Axis, Focus

**NCERT sections covered:** 9.2, 9.2.2


## Spherical mirrors (NCERT 9.2)

A steel spoon is the everyday model. Its inner, hollow side is **concave** and its outer, bulging side is **convex**. Mirrors that are a part of a sphere are **spherical mirrors**.

- **Concave mirror** — a spherical mirror whose reflecting surface is **curved inwards** (the hollow side reflects; the outside is non-reflecting).
- **Convex mirror** — a spherical mirror whose reflecting surface is **curved outwards** (the bulging side reflects).

In diagrams the slant hatching marks the non-reflecting back.

## Terms for a spherical mirror (NCERT 9.2)

1. **Pole (P)** — the centre of the reflecting surface of a spherical mirror.
2. **Centre of curvature (C)** — the centre of the sphere of which the spherical mirror is a part. Cut a hollow ball and silver one piece on the inside: that piece is a concave mirror, and the ball's centre is its C. Unlike P, **C does not lie on the mirror**. For a concave mirror C is on the reflecting side; for a convex mirror it is behind, on the non-reflecting side.
3. **Radius of curvature (R)** — the radius of the sphere of which the spherical mirror is a part. Here $PC = R$.
   - C is a **point**; R is a **length** (SI unit: metre). "Length of the centre of curvature" is a meaningless question.
4. **Principal axis** — the line passing through C and P. It is the normal to the mirror at its pole.

## Normal to a spherical surface

For a plane surface the normal is simply the perpendicular. For a spherical surface, **the normal at any point is the line through that point which, produced back, passes through the centre of curvature C** — that is, the radius at that point. The laws of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$, one plane) still hold at every point, measured from this normal.

So for a ray parallel to the principal axis, draw the normal through C. Then $\angle i = \angle r$ fixes where the reflected ray must go. On a concave mirror it bends **towards** the axis; it cannot go anywhere else.

## Principal focus and focal length (NCERT 9.2.2)

**Burning paper with a concave mirror:** point a concave mirror at the Sun and hold bits of paper in front of it. After a few minutes they catch fire. Rays from a very distant object (the Sun) arrive **parallel**, and the mirror concentrates them at one point.

- **Principal focus of a concave mirror (F)** — when parallel rays are incident on a concave mirror, after reflection they **meet** at a point on the principal axis; this point is the principal focus.
- **Principal focus of a convex mirror (F)** — when parallel rays fall on a convex mirror, after reflection they **appear to meet** (diverge from) a point behind the mirror; this point is the focus. The image idea here is *virtual*.
- **Focal length (f)** — the distance between the pole and the focus, $PF = f$.

The focus is the **midpoint of P and C**, $PF = FC$, so

$$f = \frac{R}{2}$$

(for a mirror of small aperture — proved in a later lecture).

**Drawing tip for exams (convex mirror):** mark F first, behind the mirror, and then draw each reflected ray as a straight line from F through the point where the ray meets the mirror. If C would fall off the page, move it closer, but keep $PF = FC$.

## Worked questions

**Q.** The radius of curvature of a concave mirror is $-20$ cm. Find its focal length.

$$f = \frac{R}{2} = \frac{-20}{2} = -10 \text{ cm}$$

(The minus sign comes from the sign convention, taught in a later lecture.)

**Q.** What are the focal length and radius of curvature of a plane mirror? *(Left for the class to think about.)* A plane mirror is part of a sphere of **infinite** radius, so $R = \infty$ and $f = R/2 = \infty$.
