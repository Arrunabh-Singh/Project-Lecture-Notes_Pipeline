# Refraction, Refractive Index and the Glass Slab

**NCERT sections covered:** 9.3


## Refraction (NCERT 9.3)

You walk at different speeds on concrete, in a swimming pool and on sand dunes, because your speed depends on the medium. Light behaves the same way: **its speed depends on the medium**.

**Definition (board):** when light travels obliquely from one medium to another, it **bends at the interface**. This is called **refraction of light**. The *interface* is the surface separating the two media, for example the air–water boundary.

The bending happens because light travels at different speeds in air and water. If the speeds were equal it would go straight on. A ray that strikes the interface **normally** does not bend, but refraction still occurs, because its speed still changes.

**Everyday examples**

- A rod dipped in a glass of water looks **bent** at the water surface.
- **Coin in a beaker:** place your eye where the coin is just hidden. When a friend pours in water, the coin comes into view. You now see its **apparent position**, which is raised above its **real position**.
- A swimming pool looks shallower than it is, because its floor appears raised by refraction.
- Twinkling of stars and seeing the Sun for about 4 minutes more each day (advance sunrise, delayed sunset) are also due to refraction (taken up in lecture 10).

## Optically denser and rarer media

- **Optically denser** medium: light travels **slower** in it. **Optically rarer**: light travels **faster**.
- Air and water: light is slower in water, so **air is optically rarer than water**.
- Water and glass: $v_\text{water} > v_\text{glass}$, so **water is optically rarer than glass**.

**Optical density is not mass density.** Oil floats on water: its density is 0.8 g cm⁻³ against water's 1 g cm⁻³ (mercury's is 13.6 g cm⁻³). Yet oil is **optically denser** than water: $n_\text{oil} \approx 1.4 > n_\text{water} = 1.33$.

**Summary (board):** if a medium is optically denser, its refractive index is **more** and the speed of light in it is **less**. If it is optically rarer, its refractive index is **less** and the speed is **more**.

Just as densities are quoted relative to water, refractive indices are quoted relative to **air (or vacuum), taken as 1**. "The refractive index of glass is 1.5" means 1.5 with respect to air. For water it is 1.33 (= 4/3).

**Refractive index measures how much light bends.** For the same angle of incidence, light entering glass from air bends **more** than light entering water from air, so $n_\text{glass} > n_\text{water}$.

## Direction of bending

- **Rarer → denser** (e.g. air → water): the ray bends **towards the normal**.
- **Denser → rarer** (e.g. water → air): the ray bends **away from the normal**.
- **Water ($n_1 = 1.33$) → oil ($n_2 = 1.4$):** oil is optically denser, so the ray bends **towards** the normal, even though oil is the lighter liquid.

## Absolute and relative refractive index

The refractive index of a medium with respect to air is its **absolute refractive index**:

$$n_g = n_{ga} = \frac{n_g}{n_a} = \frac{1.5}{1} = 1.5, \qquad n_w = n_{wa} = \frac{n_w}{n_a} = \frac{1.33}{1}$$

For two media neither of which is air, divide the absolute indices:

$$n_{wg} = \frac{n_w}{n_g} = \frac{n_w/n_a}{n_g/n_a} = \frac{1.33}{1.5}, \qquad n_{gw} = \frac{n_g}{n_w} = \frac{1.5}{1.33}$$

**Example.** The refractive index of diamond is 2.54 and that of glass is 1.5. What is the refractive index of glass with respect to diamond?

$$n_{gd} = \frac{n_g}{n_d} = \frac{n_g/n_a}{n_d/n_a} = \frac{1.5}{2.54} \approx 0.59$$

## Refraction through a rectangular glass slab

A ray in air meets the top face XY of a glass slab at angle of incidence $i$. Glass is denser, so the ray bends **towards** the normal; $r$ is the **angle of refraction**. Inside the slab it travels straight, because bending happens only at an interface. At the bottom face AB it goes from denser to rarer and bends **away** from the normal, leaving at the **angle of emergence** $e$. The angle inside at AB is again $r$ (alternate angles between the parallel faces).

- The bendings at XY and AB are **equal and opposite**, so $\angle i = \angle e$. The **emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray**.
- The emergent ray is shifted sideways. **Lateral displacement $d$** is the perpendicular distance between the (original) incident ray, produced forward, and the emergent ray.
- $d \propto t$, the **thickness** of the slab. Thickness is the distance between the incident face and the emergent face, so it depends on how the slab is placed. On the board, the slab standing on its long side has thickness $t_2 > t_1$, and its displacement (labelled $d_1$) is larger: $d_1 > d_2$.

## Laws of refraction (NCERT 9.3)

For light going from medium 1 (index $n_1$) to medium 2 (index $n_2$):

1. **Snell's law.** The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant, equal to the refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1:
$$\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = n_{21} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

2. The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal **all lie in the same plane**.

**Hint to avoid mixing up $n_1/n_2$:** pair each angle with the index of the medium it lies in:

$$n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r$$

- Ray from $n_4$ into $n_3$: $n_4 \sin i = n_3 \sin r$, so $\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{n_3}{n_4}$.
- Ray from $n_2$ into $n_1$: $n_2 \sin i = n_1 \sin r$, so $\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{n_1}{n_2}$.

**For the glass slab** (air $n_1$, glass $n_2$):

- At XY: $n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r$, so $\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$.
- At AB: $n_2 \sin r = n_1 \sin e$, so $\dfrac{\sin r}{\sin e} = \dfrac{n_1}{n_2}$.

Since $e = i$, the second is just the first one inverted.

## Refractive index from the speed of light

$$n = \frac{\text{speed of light in vacuum (or air)}}{\text{speed of light in the medium}}, \qquad n_{ga} = \frac{c}{v} \quad\text{(absolute refractive index)}$$

For any two media, the refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1 is the speed in medium 1 divided by the speed in medium 2:

$$n_{21} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$$

and the reverse, $n_{12} = n_1/n_2 = v_2/v_1$. The "master formula" from the lecture covers every case:

$$n_1 v_1 = n_2 v_2$$

**Q.** The speed of light is $2.5 \times 10^8$ m s⁻¹ in medium 2 and $2 \times 10^8$ m s⁻¹ in medium 3. If $n_2 = 1.5$, find $n_3$.

$$n_2 v_2 = n_3 v_3 \;\Rightarrow\; n_3 = \frac{1.5 \times 2.5 \times 10^8}{2 \times 10^8} = 1.875$$

(The lecture stops at $1.5 \times 2.5 / 2$ and leaves the arithmetic to the class.)

**Q.** The speed of light in air is $3 \times 10^8$ m s⁻¹. Find its speed in water, with $n_w = 4/3$.

$$1 \times 3 \times 10^8 = \tfrac{4}{3}\,v_2 \;\Rightarrow\; v_2 = \frac{9 \times 10^8}{4} = 2.25 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$$

*(The board's last line reads "$= 2$"; that is a slip, and the correct value is $2.25 \times 10^8$ m s⁻¹.)*
